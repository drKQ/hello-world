Earshot — a listening game for needles

Earshot

v2 · a listening game

A memory game played entirely by ear, hard enough to use up the attention that pain would otherwise take. Start it a minute before the needle and keep playing through it.

first time · learn the game

Tap when a note matches the one two notes back

You will hear four notes: low, mid, high and top. Ignore the note right before. Compare each new note with the one two back, and tap anywhere on the screen if they match.

It starts with an easier warm-up (match the note right before), then switches. If you keep missing, it slows down; if you get good, it speeds up. It's meant to be hard. That's the whole idea.

Volume—
Set your phone volume to about halfway, then slide this until the test notes are clear and comfortable, not loud. If you hear nothing at all, see the fix below.

No sound on iPhone? This version is built to play even with the silent switch on. If it's still quiet, check that your AirPods are connected and that nothing else is playing.

do this first · 15 minutes

Check if it works on you

You won't feel anything just listening. With no pain around, there's nothing for the game to take attention away from. So test it on pain you control: ice water.

  1. Fill a bowl with lots of ice and water. Wait 2 minutes.
  2. Two rounds, hand in up to the wrist: one playing the game, one in silence. The app picks the order.
  3. Pull your hand out whenever you want. It stops you at 2 minutes.
  4. About 5 minutes' rest between rounds, so your hand warms back up.

Skip this if you have heart problems, high blood pressure, Raynaud's, circulation problems or diabetes-related nerve damage, or if you're pregnant.

the real thing

Getting a shot

  1. Start a minute early, while you wait. The game needs time to pull you in.
  2. Tell the nurse you're playing a game and will keep your earbuds in. Transparency mode on.
  3. Don't watch the needle. Looking at the spot makes it hurt more.
  4. Tap Needle now when it happens (or ask the nurse to). Keep playing 30 more seconds.

For the sting itself, add something at the arm: ice on the spot for a minute beforehand, or an electric toothbrush held against the arm a few centimetres above the spot. Never on it, and ask the nurse first.

why it's built this way

A memory game, not a sound

best evidence

Pain grabs attention whenever there's spare capacity. Holding a running sequence in your head (an "n-back" task) uses that capacity up. In lab studies, people playing 1-back and 2-back games rated cold and painful stimuli as less intense, and harder versions worked better than easy ones, up to a ceiling. The effect even shows up in the spinal cord's response. Pleasant sounds or tones on their own mostly change how unpleasant pain feels, not how strong it is.

What to honestly expect

modest

This is not a numbing agent. Expect pain to feel somewhat lower and to bother you less. Lab studies show ratings drop more reliably than endurance does; in one, pain-free people kept a hand in cold water about a quarter longer while distracted. A single shot is also over in a second, so the game has to be running and absorbing you before the needle arrives, not started at the last moment.

The ear can't block the nerve

add the arm

Vibration and cold numb a jab only when they're close to it, because they work on the same nerve path in the spinal cord. Sound in your ear can't do that; it works through attention alone. So the strongest setup is both: something cold or buzzing near the arm for the sting, and the game in your ears for your attention.

Look away

free

Paying attention to a body part turns pain there up; attending elsewhere turns it down. Closing your eyes and listening hard is a good combination.

Not a medical device. A distraction aid built from published research. It makes no diagnostic or treatment claim. Keep Transparency on so you can hear the person giving the injection, and take your earbuds out if they ask.
